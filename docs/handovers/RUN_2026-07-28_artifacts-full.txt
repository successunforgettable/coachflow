ICP  (id 259)  — Exhausted First-Time Parents
==============================================================================

--- introduction ---
I am somewhere between functioning and falling apart. My baby is seven months old and I genuinely cannot remember the last time I slept more than two hours in a row. I love her more than I knew was possible, and I also fantasise about sleeping in a hotel room alone for an entire weekend. Both of those things are true at the same time and I feel guilty even typing that.

My days have this strange blurry quality. I make coffee and forget I made it. I lose words mid-sentence. I cried last Tuesday because the lid wouldn't come off the pasta jar. My partner and I are snapping at each other over nothing — or actually over everything, because we're both running on empty and there's nobody to take the hit for us. I've read approximately four thousand articles about infant sleep. I've tried the 90-minute wake window, the Huckleberry app schedule, the blackout blinds, the white noise machine turned up louder than a vacuum cleaner. I bought a sleep sack. Then a different sleep sack. I've done the dream feed. I've dropped the dream feed. Nothing sticks for more than three nights before we're back to square one.

What I really need — what I'm desperate for but slightly afraid to say out loud — is someone who actually knows what they're doing to just tell me what to do. Step by step. For my baby, not some hypothetical average baby from a study. I want to stop Googling at 2am. I want to stop feeling like I'm failing at the most basic thing a parent is supposed to figure out. I want my baby to be well-rested and happy, and I want to lie down in my own bed and stay there until morning. I want my life back — or at least a version of it that doesn't feel like a continuous emergency.

--- fears ---
• I lie awake worrying that I've already created a habit so ingrained that no one can fix it — that I've "ruined" my baby's sleep permanently by feeding her to sleep every single time for the past six months and now she physically cannot do it any other way.
• I lie awake worrying that if I try anything new, it's going to make things worse — we had three okay nights last week and then it all collapsed, and now I'm terrified to change anything in case I trigger another regression.
• I lie awake worrying that I'm damaging her emotionally. I've read things about cortisol and attachment and I can't unread them, and now every time she cries even for a second I feel this spike of panic that I am actively harming her brain development.
• I lie awake worrying that my partner is starting to quietly resent me — like somehow this is my fault for not being tougher about sleep training from the beginning, and we're not saying it to each other but it's sitting there between us.
• I lie awake worrying that the sleep deprivation is making me a worse mother than I would otherwise be — that I'm so foggy and short-tempered that she's picking up on my anxiety and that's part of why she won't settle.
• I lie awake worrying that I'll spend money on a sleep consultant and nothing will change — that my baby is just one of those babies that doesn't sleep, and I'll have wasted money we don't really have on something that works for other families but not mine.
• I lie awake worrying that other mums in my antenatal group have figured this out and aren't telling me something, and that I'm the only one still doing night feeds at seven months while everyone else's babies have been sleeping through since four months.

--- hopesDreams ---
• The specific morning where I wake up, look at my phone, and it says 6:47am — and I realise I don't remember the last time I was awake. I lie there for a second just checking it's real. That morning.
• A version of bedtime that takes forty-five minutes instead of two and a half hours — where I do the bath, the feed, the song, I put her down awake, I walk out of the room, and she actually goes to sleep. Every night. Predictably.
• Getting to sit on the sofa with my partner after 8pm and watch something on TV that is not a sleep training forum thread, and just feel like a couple again instead of two exhausted co-workers on the same terrible shift.
• Going back to work in six weeks without feeling like I'm already running a deficit — showing up with an actual functioning brain, being able to hold a thought through a whole meeting, not counting down the hours until I can sit down.
• My baby waking up from her nap in a genuinely good mood — not overtired and screaming — because she's actually sleeping enough and her whole nervous system has had a chance to reset.
• Being able to go to my next mothers' group catch-up and say honestly "yeah, sleep is actually fine now" instead of doing the dark-humour deflection I've perfected to avoid the conversation.
• A long weekend away with my partner — not tomorrow, but in six months — knowing that whoever is watching the baby has a routine that works, that bedtime won't be a disaster, that the sleep we've built will travel.

--- psychographics ---
She is the kind of person who, before the baby, approached problems by researching them thoroughly and then executing a plan. She has a bookshelf that is half fiction half non-fiction. She had a birth plan. She read the pregnancy books with a highlighter. She is not someone who gives up easily, which is also why the sleep situation is so destabilising — she is accustomed to effort producing results, and here she is, six months in, with nothing to show for it except a stack of notes from a Huckleberry app and a body that's stopped trusting itself.

She has strong opinions about parenting, but she holds them anxiously rather than loudly. She believes in responsiveness. She believes the cry-it-out method is not for her family — not because she looked it up once, but because she has read the actual research, she has opinions about attachment theory, she follows specific paediatricians and developmental psychologists on Instagram, and she has had the conversation with her partner and they are aligned: they do not want their baby screaming alone in a dark room. This makes sleep training feel like a completely closed door to her, and she needs someone to show her that there is another door.

When she has a rare hour to herself she is probably scrolling — TikTok parenting accounts, Instagram sleep advice reels, Reddit threads in r/beyondthebump or r/sleeptrain — not for entertainment but because she is still trying to solve the problem. She doesn't fully switch off. Her version of switching off is a hot shower that lasts longer than three minutes, a podcast she's been half-listening to for two weeks, or a glass of wine on the sofa at 9pm before she falls asleep sitting up. She is exhausted in a way that has seeped into her personality.

She trusts experts, but specifically experts who speak to her values. She would not buy a program from someone who told her sleep training was just letting them cry. She responds to warm authority — someone who clearly knows more than she does AND seems to genuinely care about the wellbeing of the baby AND doesn't make her feel judged for not having figured it out yet. She is also slightly suspicious of anyone who makes it sound too easy, because she has been let down by "it only took three nights!" promises before.

--- pains ---
• Every night between 6pm and 8:30pm, she spends up to two hours trying to get the baby down — feeding, rocking, putting her in the cot, watching her eyes flicker open the second her back touches the mattress, starting again — while the rest of the evening disappears.
• Every time she thinks she has cracked a nap schedule, the baby skips a nap entirely for no visible reason and the entire rest of the day implodes because now she's overtired at bedtime and nothing works.
• Every morning she wakes up having been up two or three times, adds it up in her head, and realises she has had about four hours total — then has to function as a parent and a human being on that amount.
• Every week she reads a new piece of advice that directly contradicts something she read the week before — wake windows versus feed-to-sleep versus drowsy-but-awake — and she has no way to know which one is actually right for her baby.
• Every time she mentions how bad sleep is to someone outside her immediate circle, she gets either unsolicited advice she's already tried ("have you tried a dream feed?") or dismissal ("it's just a phase, enjoy it while it lasts") and neither response helps.
• Every Sunday night she does a low-grade dread calculation — how bad will this week be, how many night wakings, how will she get through work — and it sits in her chest like a stone.
• Every time her baby has a few good nights in a row and she starts to feel hopeful, something disrupts it — a cold, a developmental leap, a trip to visit family — and they are back at the beginning, which makes her feel like progress isn't real.
• Every time she sees an Instagram reel of a mum doing a soft bedtime routine and the baby just... going to sleep, she feels a flash of something between longing and shame that she cannot replicate that in her own home.
• Every month that passes, she is more acutely aware that the sleep deprivation is compounding — it is not a phase she is pushing through, it is a sustained state of depletion that is affecting her mood, her memory, her relationship, and her ability to enjoy her baby.

--- frustrations ---
• WHY does it seem like the exact same technique works for everyone in the Facebook group except me — they post "day three and she's sleeping 7 to 7!" and I try the same thing and night one is a disaster and I abandon it, and I don't know if I gave up too soon or if it just isn't right for us.
• WHY does every sleep resource give me a framework but nobody actually tells me what to DO when the baby wakes at 1am and won't go back down — the gap between "here's the theory" and "here's what to do right now at this specific moment" is enormous and nobody fills it.
• WHY does my baby seem to specifically resist every intervention at the exact moment I've started believing it might work — three good nights, then a night that's worse than before we started, and I feel like I'm being gaslit by a nine-kilogram person.
• WHY is there so much conflicting advice from people who all sound equally authoritative — one pediatric sleep "expert" on Instagram says one thing, another says the direct opposite, and I have no framework for figuring out who is actually right.
• WHY do people act like loving your baby means you should be fine with never sleeping — like if I say I need to fix this, I'm admitting I don't want to be present for her, when actually I want to fix it BECAUSE I want to be more present for her.
• WHY is it that the moment I have spent forty-five minutes settling her to sleep and I finally sit down, I hear the first sounds of her waking again — every single time, it's like she can hear the sofa cushion compress.
• WHY does my own mother make me feel like I'm overcomplicating something that "used to just happen naturally" — as if I have somehow made this harder than it needs to be, when actually I am trying everything and nothing is working.

--- goals ---
• In three weeks, my baby is going down in her cot awake, with minimal protest, and sleeping a continuous stretch of at least six hours — I have this as a concrete milestone, not a vague hope.
• I stop Googling "baby won't sleep" and "8 month sleep regression" and "how to break feed to sleep association" after 10pm — I have a plan, I trust it, I don't need to keep searching.
• My partner and I have a functional evening again — we eat dinner together, we watch something, we go to bed at a reasonable hour — and I feel like we're on the same team instead of two people managing a crisis.
• I go back to work without the sleep deprivation being the first and most consuming thing I think about — I am not lying in meetings calculating how many hours I got last night.
• I can hand my baby to a grandparent or a babysitter for a night with an actual written routine that works, and I trust that bedtime will not be a catastrophe in my absence.
• Within six months I feel like myself again — not the pre-baby version, but a rested version of the mother I actually want to be, where I have enough in the tank to be patient and playful and present.
• I reach a point where when someone in my mothers' group mentions sleep problems, I can say "we went through that, here's what actually helped us" — and mean it from the other side.

--- values ---
• Responsiveness as love — I believe that responding to my baby's cues is fundamental to building a secure attachment, which means any method that requires me to ignore her crying feels like a betrayal of that value, even when I'm desperate. This makes me resistant to anything that sounds even adjacent to sleep training.
• Evidence over instinct, but only the evidence that confirms my instinct — I tell myself I want to follow the research, but I've already filtered out any research that supports methods I'm uncomfortable with. I need the approach I buy into to have citations AND feel right, and that's a narrow target.
• Self-sufficiency as competence — I have a strong underlying belief that good mothers figure things out, and paying someone to help me with something as "basic" as getting my baby to sleep feels like admitting I've failed, even though I would never apply that logic to someone else.
• Not wanting to be sold to — I have a significant aversion to anything that feels like a sales pitch. If I sense I'm being marketed at, I close the tab. I need to feel like I found the answer, not that someone found me with an ad.
• Protecting the baby above everything — my primary filter for every decision is "is this safe and kind for her?" which means I will not move forward with any approach until I am completely convinced it will not cause her distress. My worry about her trumps my own suffering, every time.
• Fairness and value — I am on maternity pay or recently returned to work on a reduced schedule, and I feel the weight of spending money on something that isn't a guaranteed fix. I need to feel the price is proportionate before I will act.

--- objections ---
• "I'm not sure I can afford it right now." — "I've already bought two sleep books and a course I never finished, and if I spend money on this and it doesn't work for my specific baby, I will feel like an idiot and we genuinely can't afford to waste it."
• "I want to look into it a bit more before deciding." — "I'm not convinced you're different from the last five things I tried. I need evidence this isn't another generic method that works for 'most babies' but not mine."
• "I need to talk to my partner first." — "My partner is skeptical that paying for a sleep consultant is necessary and I don't feel confident enough in this yet to convince them. If I'm going to go to bat for spending this money, I need to be more certain than I currently am."
• "I'm worried about the method — I don't want anything that involves crying." — "I'm not totally sure I trust that a 'gentle' method described by someone I found through an ad will actually be gentle. I need to see exactly what it involves before I commit, because if it turns out to be cry-it-out in a different font, I'll feel deceived."
• "I'm not sure this is the right time — we have a trip coming up / she's in a leap / we've just had a disruption." — "I'm scared to start something and fail again. If I wait for a perfect window, I never have to find out whether this would have worked."
• "I've heard mixed things about sleep consultants." — "I don't know how to tell a certified, experienced person from someone who did a weekend course and now charges for what I could find on Google. I need to understand why you specifically know what you're doing."

--- communicationStyle ---
She communicates best when she feels like she's talking to someone who has genuinely been inside this problem — not someone who is performing empathy from a script. She reads tone very carefully. She will notice within a sentence or two whether you are talking to her like a person or to her like a demographic. She needs warmth before authority. Lead with acknowledgement of how hard this is before you say anything about your credentials or your method.

What turns her off immediately: clinical language, overpromising ("your baby will sleep through the night — GUARANTEED"), anything that sounds like a funnel. She has been on enough Instagram accounts and in enough Facebook groups to have a highly developed filter for people who are performing expertise rather than demonstrating it. She will not convert from a single ad. She needs to encounter your thinking — a real tip that works, a real explanation of why something happens, a voice that sounds like it knows something — before she will take a next step.

She responds to specificity over generality. "Here's why your baby wakes up 45 minutes into every nap" will hold her attention. "Help your baby sleep better" will lose her in two seconds. She wants to feel like you are describing her situation so accurately that you must have been in her house.

She trusts people who cite reasons, not just results. She wants to know WHY the method works, not just that it does. She engages with educational content first — a reel that explains something she didn't know, a post that names the exact scenario she's in — and moves toward trust when that content is consistent and feels credible. She prefers DMs or emails over phone calls, especially in this season of life when a phone call with a crying baby in the background feels impossible. She will read a long caption if the first line has hooked her. She will not watch a 45-minute webinar unless she already trusts the person.

# The Voice Problem: Why Copy Sounds Like an Institution and How to Make It Sound Like a Person

When organizations sit down to write, a strange transformation occurs. Clear, warm-blooded human beings suddenly start writing like faceless, bureaucratic boards [320]. This is "the voice problem": the reflexive default to an abstract, clinical, and detached register that drains copy of its persuasive energy [399]. Most business copy reads like it was written by robots—stiff, formal, and about as engaging as watching paint dry [320].

This guide breaks down what specifically makes copy sound like a person rather than an institution at the level of word choice, sentence construction, and what the writer chooses to notice.

---

## 1. The Institutional Wall: Why We Write Like Corporations

The default to institutional prose is driven by psychological barriers [33, 262]. Many business owners and writers harbor a deep-seated belief that professional writing must be formal [328]. This high-school-derived reflex causes us to clutter our sentences to sound "scholarly" or important [179, 370]. In corporate environments, turgid language is often used as a defensive shield; writers believe a simple style reflects a simple mind, whereas complex language conveys expertise [262]. In reality, a simple style is the result of hard work and clear thinking, while a muddled, complex style reflects a muddled thinker or someone too lazy to organize their thoughts [251, 262].

This stylistic choice has immediate neurological consequences for how readers receive marketing copy [33]. When copy tries too hard to sell or wraps its message in a harsh, clinical, or pushy tone, the reader’s lizard brain registers an alert [33]. The amygdala flags the copy as a "threat" (a high-pressure sales pitch) and triggers a defensive response [33]. Conversely, when a message is delivered in a friendly, conversational, and understanding tone, the amygdala stays quiet and the prefrontal cortex lights up [33]. This is the part of the brain that manages social behavior, personality expression, and—most importantly—decision-making [33].

Writing is not a dry broadcasting of features; it is an "intimate transaction" between two real people conducted on paper [256]. The ultimate product any writer has to sell is not the subject being written about, but who they are [252, 369]. When an organization refuses to be clear and personal in its writing, it loses trust, friends, and customers [261]. To break down the institutional wall, the writer must relax, find confidence, and write as if they are speaking directly to a close friend over coffee [256, 325, 329].

## 2. Word Choice: The Suffixes of Stodginess vs. The Vocabulary of Humans

The most prominent difference between a person and an institution is word choice. Stuffy copy relies on noun-heavy structures that bury actions; human copy uses simple, muscular verbs that drive sentences forward [23, 244].

### The Nominalization Trap

Linguistic analysts identify **nominalization**—the grammatical process of converting a verb or adjective into an abstract noun—as the primary engine of lifeless prose [179, 400]. When "analyze" becomes "analysis," "decide" becomes "decision," or "improve" becomes "improvement," the sentence loses its kinetic energy [231, 400]. 

Nominalizations are "nounified" words that end in predictable, heavy suffixes such as *-tion, -sion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -cy, -ance,* and *-ence* [135, 232, 278]. While useful for naming broad theoretical concepts (like "alienation" or "calibration") [278, 280], overusing them in copy forces the writer to introduce weak, empty connecting verbs (e.g., *be, has, seems, makes, provides, gives, conducts, experiences*) to hold the sentence together [142, 187, 400]. 

For example, instead of writing "the committee decided," an institutional writer will write "the committee *made a decision*" [136, 207]. Joseph Williams outlines a simple three-step process to dismantle this pattern [141]:
1. **Diagnose**: Locate abstract nouns, particularly those in the subject position or trailing empty verbs [141, 142].
2. **Analyze**: Identify the true human actor and the actual action being performed [141].
3. **Rewrite**: Change the nominalization back into an active verb, make the human the subject, and restructure the sentence [141].

### Preposition Pile-Ups and Qualifying Dilution

Chains of nominalizations inevitably spawn **preposition pile-ups** [144]. Because abstract nouns cannot act on each other directly, they must be linked by strings of prepositions like *of, in, for, by,* and *through* [144, 183]. A sentence like "The *evaluation of* the performance and the *analysis of* the results led to the *formulation of* a solution" is sluggish and complex [144]. Collapsing the nominalizations and eliminating the prepositions clears the "fog" instantly: "We evaluated the performance, analyzed the results, and formulated a solution" [144, 272].

Furthermore, institutional copy is plagued by **qualifying dilution** [401]. Out of a lack of confidence or an excess of caution, corporate writers pepper their drafts with qualifiers like *sort of, kind of, very, quite, rather, pretty much, a bit of,* and *actually* [26, 291, 401]. These words do not strengthen statements; they weaken them and diminish the writer's authority [26, 430]. Strunk and White argue that copy is more powerful when it stands with quiet, unqualified certainty [26, 408]. If you are confused, say "I am confused," not "I am *kind of* confused" [430]. 

### Anglo-Saxon Verbs over Latinate Jargon

Corporate prose prefers Latinate, polysyllabic jargon because it sounds "erudite" [17, 298]. Humans, however, use simple Anglo-Saxon words [31, 119]. To write like a person, choose simple, familiar terms over pretentious professionalese [31, 391]:
* Use **"use"** instead of "utilize" [31, 390].
* Use **"help"** instead of "facilitate" or "assist" [31, 188].
* Use **"show"** instead of "demonstrate" [31, 139].
* Use **"get"** instead of "acquire" or "experience" [99, 133].

Using simple, straightforward language does not make copy look unprofessional or unsophisticated [71]. Clever readers will easily understand simple language, while average readers will struggle with clever language [78]. By keeping it simple, you appeal to both crowds without offending either [78].

## 3. Sentence Construction: Cadence, Punctuation, and Breaking the Rules

Institutional prose moves at a plodding, uniform gait that puts readers to sleep [257]. Human speech, by contrast, has rhythm, pauses, abrupt shifts, and rules that exist only to be broken [10, 35].

### The Breath Test and Varying Sentence Length

William Zinsser writes entirely by ear and reads everything aloud before letting it go out into the world [257]. Conversational copywriters emphasize that **reading your copy aloud at the kitchen table** is the definitive test of human voice [404]. If you run out of breath before reaching a period, the sentence is too long [308, 404]. If your partner cringes when they hear it, you need to rewrite it [172]. 

A conversational cadence relies on varying your sentence lengths [404]. A long, descriptive sentence that mirrors a complex, flowing thought should be followed by an occasional short sentence [239, 404]. Short sentences create a tremendous punch and provide sweet "eye relief" on the page [122, 239]. Split or shorten sentences when they exceed 14 to 20 words to keep copy scannable and maintain reading momentum [4].

### The Punctuation of Speech

Formal copy adheres to strict punctuation rules, relying heavily on colons and semicolons [11]. In conversational writing, semicolons are "distracting" because most readers do not know what they do [11]. To write like a person, replace stuffy punctuation with the markers of natural speech [10]:
* **The Em-Dash (—)**: Connects associative ideas without starting a new sentence, mirroring how thoughts naturally leap together when we talk [10, 405].
* **The Ellipsis (...)**: Invites the reader to lean in and signals a natural, intimate pause [10, 405].
* **Parentheses ( )**: Act as whispered, behind-the-scenes comments or asides between friends, creating a warm, conspiratorial subtext [10, 405].
* **No Period after Headlines**: Avoid putting periods after headlines or crossheads to keep the thought open and encourage the reader to glide straight into the body copy [11].

### Rule-Breaking as humanity

To sound human, you must let go of rigid classroom dogmas [270, 328]. Conversational copywriters freely break formal rules to achieve a natural flow [170]:
* **Start sentences with conjunctions** (*And, But, Because*) to transition smoothly between thoughts [171, 270].
* **Use contractions** (*you're, didn't, hasn't*) religiously [71]. As copywriter Harry Dry notes, "Only academics say 'you are'" [171].
* **Split infinitives** (*"to boldly go"*) to avoid awkward, stuffy word order and place adverbs next to the verbs they actually modify [364].
* **Load up on personal pronouns** (*I, you, we, us*) [34, 237]. Address your reader directly as "you" and refer to yourself as "I" or "we" [34, 78]. This creates a personal, human-to-human relationship instead of treating the reader as "the customer" or "the user" [78].

## 4. What the Writer Chooses to Notice: Specificity over Category

An institution notices categories; a person notices details. Stuffy writing relies on abstract summaries that require the reader to do the heavy mental translation work [402]. Human writing uses concrete, specific language to engage the reader’s imagination [154, 316].

### The Specificity Sweep

To move from abstract claims to immediate trust, a copywriter must perform what Joanna Wiebe calls a **"Specificity Sweep"** [403]. Go through your rough draft and relentlessly replace generalized "category nouns" with sensory-rich details [152, 403]. 
* Instead of "a period of unfavorable weather," write **"it rained for a week"** [24, 265].
* Instead of "we experienced performance issues," write **"page loads spiked to 5 seconds during peak traffic"** [32].
* Instead of "substantial savings," write **"save $420 a year"** [50, 77].

Claude Hopkins, the father of modern advertising, demonstrated this principle decades ago [116]. While competitor brands were screaming generalized claims of "purity," Hopkins made his copy highly persuasive by stating a specific fact: "Our bottles are washed with live steam" [116, 402]. Specific details feel credible and urgent because they imply a verifiable mechanism rather than generalized marketing hype [50, 333].

### Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) Customer Language

A person writes to a single, real client avatar, not a generic "demographic" [118, 413]. To speak in a voice that resonates, you must understand what Clayton Christensen termed the **"Jobs-to-be-Done" (JTBD)** perspective [407]. Customers do not buy products for their features; they "hire" them to solve specific "moments of struggle" in their lives [357, 358].

To capture this, do not brainstorm copy in an echo chamber [361]. Instead, record and transcribe interviews with your actual customers [6, 330]. The raw transcript will contain the exact, unpolished words they use to describe their frustrations and desires [6, 360]. Mirror these exact phrases back to them [360]. By describing their quiet "moment of struggle" with extreme accuracy, you bypass their natural skepticism [361, 407]. The reader instantly feels understood and reasons: *"If this person can describe my struggle so perfectly, they must also understand the path to resolve it"* [407].

## 5. Worked Before-and-After Rewrites

Here are three detailed before-and-after rewrites applying these principles across B2B, SaaS, and solo-practitioner service marketing.

### Rewrite 1: B2B Operational Copy
*Focusing on dismantling preposition pile-ups, empty verbs, and restoring the active voice.*

* **Before (Institutional)**: 
  > "The implementation of our proprietary digital document management system facilitates the optimization of operational efficiency and results in the substantial minimization of manual administrative labor for your personnel." [38 words]
* **After (Conversational)**: 
  > "Our digital document system helps your team get more done in less time, freeing them from hours of tedious manual paperwork." [21 words]

#### Step-by-Step Breakdown:
1. **Dismantling Nominalizations**: The nouns *implementation, optimization, efficiency,* and *minimization* were converted back into their active verb and adjective counterparts (*helps, get done, freeing*) [11, 408].
2. **Eliminating Empty Verbs**: The empty, weak verb phrase *facilitates the optimization of* was collapsed into the simple active verb **"helps"** [31, 142].
3. **Clearing Preposition Pile-Ups**: The string *of our proprietary digital document management system... of operational efficiency... of manual administrative labor* was entirely dissolved, replacing abstract categories with concrete nouns (*document system, paperwork*) [11, 144].
4. **Shifting to the Human Character**: The abstract subject *The implementation* was replaced by **"Our digital document system"** acting on a concrete character: **"your team"** [275, 408].

---

### Rewrite 2: SaaS Value Proposition
*Focusing on the "Specificity Sweep" and moving from features to the customer's "moment of struggle."*

* **Before (Institutional)**: 
  > "We deliver state-of-the-art automated financial reconciliation methodologies designed for the mitigation of accounting discrepancies and the maximization of fiscal oversight." [21 words]
* **After (Conversational)**: 
  > "Stop spending your Sunday nights hunting down missing $15 receipts. Our software reconciles your accounts automatically so you can sleep easy." [21 words]

#### Step-by-Step Breakdown:
1. **Unpacking the Summary**: The abstract category *financial reconciliation methodologies* was unpacked into a vivid, relatable scene: **"spending Sunday nights hunting down missing $15 receipts"** [155, 359].
2. **Identifying the JTBD Moment of Struggle**: The copy moves from a dry feature description to the customer's actual frustration (losing weekends to accounting anxiety) and their desired outcome (**"so you can sleep easy"**) [358, 407].
3. **Active Voice and Pronouns**: Replaced *designed for the mitigation of...* with **"Our software reconciles... automatically"**, speaking directly to **"you"** [34, 417].
4. **Ditching Pretentious Jargon**: Replaced Latinate jargon like *fiscal oversight* and *mitigation of discrepancies* with simple, clear concepts [31, 391].

---

### Rewrite 3: Relational Practitioner Lead-in (Somatic Coach)
*Focusing on quiet certainty, removing qualifying dilution, and replacing concept nouns with emotional specificity.*

* **Before (Institutional)**: 
  > "There is a common experience among high-performing individuals of feeling a sort of emotional exhaustion, which often manifests as a state of physical tension. A therapeutic intervention centered on somatic awareness can provide facilitation for the release of these blocks." [39 words]
* **After (Conversational)**: 
  > "You are exhausted. Your jaw is clenched, your shoulders are tight, and you're carrying your work anxiety home in your body. Let's work together to release that tension and help you breathe again." [32 words]

#### Step-by-Step Breakdown:
1. **Removing Qualifying Dilution**: Stripped out the defensive, weakening qualifier *a sort of* [26, 401, 408]. The statement **"You are exhausted"** now stands with quiet, undeniable certainty [408].
2. **Erasing Empty Sentence Openings**: Replaced the turgid, passive opening *There is a common experience...* with a direct, personal address: **"You are exhausted"** [143, 170].
3. **Concept Nouns to Concrete Sensory Experience**: The abstract nouns *emotional exhaustion* and *physical tension* were translated into concrete, somatic details the reader can actually feel: **"Your jaw is clenched, your shoulders are tight..."** [359, 375].
4. **Pronoun Shift**: Replaced the clinical third-person reference (*high-performing individuals, a therapeutic intervention*) with **"you"**, **"your"**, and **"us"** [34, 417]. The copywriter is no longer speaking *at* the reader, but having a warm, sincere conversation *with* them [69, 78].
